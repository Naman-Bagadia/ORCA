# ORCA: Oil Removal and Collection Assistant

## Technical Abstract

Cleaning oil from water involves more than spotting a dark patch on the surface. The oil can spread, move into difficult-to-reach corners and become harder to collect. For someone working from the bank of a pond or lake, even reaching the affected area can be a challenge. ORCA is our attempt to make that task more manageable: a compact robotic boat or unmanned surface vehicle to be specific, designed to help an operator inspect the water surface and collect floating oil using a removable absorbent sponge called polyurethane.

At the centre of ORCA is an **ESP32 DevKit**, which coordinates the boat's movement, receives wireless commands and reads connected sensors. The propulsion system uses **1 12V DC motor and propeller**, controlled through an **L298N motor driver**. Steering is handled separately by **1 MG995 servo attached to a rudder**. This arrangement lets us investigate how propulsion and steering can work together to bring the collection material into contact with a contaminated patch without unnecessarily disturbing the water.

The boat communicates with its ground station through **2 LoRa (long range) modules operating at 433 MHz**, with one module at each end. The link is intended to carry movement commands and selected sensor or status readings, not live video. Manual control is our starting point because it allows an operator to judge the surroundings while we test the boat's reliability. Regulated **5 V and 3.3 V power rails**, alongside a dedicated supply suited to the servo, support the electronics. The power arrangement will accommodate changing motor and servo loads without interrupting the controller.

For visual monitoring, the updated design retains the **ESP32-CAM** and adds the **original HUSKYLENS 1** for computer vision-based oil detection and navigation. The initial task is to learn **2 classes: clean water and water with visible signs of oil** via a machine learning model for classification, trained on **Edge Impulse.** HUSKYLENS shows a live camera feed and sends its results to the main ESP32 through I2C. However, teaching a camera to distinguish sample images is only the beginning. Reflections, shadows, ripples and floating debris can produce misleading appearances, so testing must include unfamiliar scenes rather than only the examples used during learning. We therefore describe its output as **suspected oil**, not confirmed contamination. Once there is a prospect of oil on the water, the robot autonomously moves towards the source recorded on the camera for further analysis.

A **turbidity sensor** adds another source of information and confirmation of oil by measuring the clarity and opacity of the water. Its readings can help us assess if the water has an oil spill by measuring the cloudiness and haziness of the water. However, cloudy water is not necessarily oily, and an oil film may exist on otherwise clear water. For this reason, turbidity and visual classification are treated as different observations, not interchangeable proof of an oil spill. They co-exist together as features to prevent false positives.

The proposed collection material is a removable **polyurethane (PU) sponge**. We are interested in a material that attracts oil while taking up as little water as possible. Research shows what specialised polyurethane treatments can achieve: Argonne National Laboratory's engineered **Oleo Sponge has been reported to absorb up to 90 times its own weight in oil**. That figure belongs to the specially modified research material; it is **not a tested result for ORCA's sponge**, and ordinary PU foam should not be assumed to perform similarly.

Our testing will focus on how much oil the selected sponge collects, how much water it retains, whether it holds the oil during movement and how easily it can be replaced. Collection capacity will be reported in **grams of oil per gram of dry sponge**, with water uptake assessed separately. We also plan to record collection time and changes in performance after reuse.

ORCA's contribution is the team's integration of these elements into a student-built cleanup platform. Contained-tank trials will help establish what works, what remains unreliable and what needs redesigning. Rather than presenting ORCA as a replacement for professional spill-response equipment, we aim to demonstrate a useful working principle and a practical starting point for further development.
