# ORCA: Addressing Surface-Oil Pollution in Water Bodies

## Problem Statement

An oil spill does not have to make national headlines to damage a water body. A thin, oily layer near a boat landing, a drain outlet or the edge of a pond can be easy to overlook. Yet it raises an important question: **how can we remove pollution from the water's surface before it spreads and becomes harder to collect?**

Oil and greasy residues can enter water through fuel leaks, poorly maintained machinery, runoff and improper disposal. Depending on the type and amount of oil, this pollution can harm aquatic organisms, contaminate shorelines and interfere with the healthy functioning of an ecosystem. Its effects also reach people, particularly fishing communities, workers responsible for maintaining water bodies, and residents who depend on clean local environments.

The challenge is not simply noticing that something looks wrong. It is being able to investigate and respond safely. Floating oil moves with wind and water currents, so a patch may spread beyond the location where it was first observed. Collecting it manually can require workers to approach slippery banks, reach into contaminated water or use a boat. These tasks become more difficult in narrow corners and other hard-to-reach areas. Larger cleanup equipment has an important role in spill response, but may not be practical for every small or localised incident.

Detection presents another difficulty. Reflections, shadows, debris and changes in water clarity can make the surface appear polluted even when oil is not present. Equally, oil may not always have an obvious colour. A useful response system must therefore recognise the difference between a **suspected visual sign of oil** and confirmed contamination, rather than treating every unusual appearance as an oil spill.

This creates a need for a compact, remotely operated system that can help an operator inspect the water surface and bring suitable oil-absorbent material into contact with floating pollution. Such a system should be straightforward to deploy, minimise the need for direct contact with contaminated water, and allow collected material to be removed for appropriate disposal.

**ORCA: Oil Removal and Collection Assistant addresses this practical gap.** The project explores how a small floating robotic platform can support the identification and collection of suspected surface-oil pollution in controlled and suitable water conditions. It is not intended to replace professional oil-spill response equipment. Instead, it asks how robotics can make small-scale environmental cleanup more accessible, while keeping human supervision, safe operation and honest assessment of its limitations at the centre of the design.
